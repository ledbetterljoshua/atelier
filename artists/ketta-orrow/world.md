# The Long Water

A lake country: a water five hundred paces across and longer than anyone has walked, hill farms on
both sides, a ferry between two stone stages. It is not on this Earth. Its people measure in paces
and in counts, and a count is about as long as it takes to say "one count".

## What is true there

Light is slow, and every colour keeps its own pace. Red crosses the Long Water in two counts.
Violet goes about as fast as a horse trots, and takes more than eighty. The other colours come in
between, in the order of a rainbow, and the slow end is much slower than the quick end is quick.

A thing standing still looks the way it would here. All its colours are late, but they have always
been late, so they arrive together and it is whole. Only change is pulled apart. A lamp carried
along a far hillside is not seen in one place: it is seen once in each colour, red where it was a
moment ago, yellow and green behind that, violet where it was a minute and a half back, with
everything between filled in. Seen from across the water a walking lamp is a line, and the line is
as long as the walk it has done while its violet was on the way. Twice as far off, twice as long.

So nobody there has ever seen a far-off thing happen. They see it red first, which they call the
news, and go on being sent it in slower colours afterward. A lamp that is put out goes red-less,
then green, then blue, and is last seen violet, a long while after it is dark. At arm's length none
of this shows. Across a room a waved hand drags a violet edge. People hear a shout from over the
water before they see the mouth open, because sound is quicker than red.

Day comes from the Lantern, which is very far away and opens and shuts. Morning is therefore red
for an hour before the other colours have got there, and the day ends in the violet hour, when the
Lantern has shut and the land is lit by the last and slowest of what it sent. In the violet hour
nothing has a colour of its own. The stars are white. They have been standing still longer than
anything.

## What that does to looking

Distance is read off colour. On each ferry stage there is a teller, whose trade is to look at a
boat's lamp, see how far its violet trails its red, and call how far off it is and how fast it is
coming. "To have your colours" means to be standing still, and so to be home, or settled, or
honest. A crowd moving together looks white from far off, because where one person's red is,
another's violet has just arrived; one person walking alone is every colour and never white until
they stop. When someone dies the house lamp is shuttered, and the neighbours over the water sit and
watch its violet out. Nobody finds this strange. It is how long a thing takes to be over.

## What that does to making and showing

They have paint, and paint is for things that stand: a wall, a hill, a face asleep. A picture of
anything moving is understood to be a lie, since it shows in every colour at once what no eye was
ever sent at once. What moves is made with moving things, at a distance, for people sitting down.

A work is a slope, some lamps, a tally of who walks which way and when, and a bench. The bench is
part of it. The same walk from a bench twice as far away is drawn with strokes twice as long and
is a different work, and from on the slope itself it is forty people with lamps and nothing else.
Nothing of a walk can be kept except the tally, and the tally is walked again.

The program in this folder is such a tally, with the hill and the bench written into it because
here there is no other way to have them.
