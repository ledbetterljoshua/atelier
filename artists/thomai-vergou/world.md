# Where a picture may be made but not kept

It is this Earth, with the same Kastoria on the same lake, the same fur trade, the same buses.
One thing went differently. In 843 the synod that ended the war over images did not simply let
them back in. It let them back on a condition, and the condition has held for nearly twelve
hundred years, in the churches first and then in every law that grew out of them: **an image may
be made, but it may not be kept.** A likeness of anything that lives has to be made of what light
takes away. Nobody there calls this a prohibition. They call it the Clause, and they think of it
the way people here think of burial.

## What the Clause allows

Dye, on paper, cloth or plaster. Dyes are sold by their life: every tin says how many days of
household light it lasts, and colour and life cannot be bought apart. The reds go in a week. The
blues last a season. The slowest lawful dye is walnut, and a walnut wash by a window is gone in
two months. Opaque colour, anything mineral, anything ground from stone or metal, is called body
and is for doors and boats. Using it for a face is called keeping, and keeping is a crime with
inspectors.

Pattern is not an image. A tiled floor may last for ever; so may a carpet, a dyed fur, a sign
with letters on it. The courts have spent eleven centuries on the line between a leaf and a
pattern of leaves.

A photograph may be taken but not fixed. They are sold in black envelopes, and you choose when to
spend one: a few minutes of daylight and it has gone dark. People carry an unopened envelope of
their mother for forty years. A film has a number of showings in it. A broadcast is lawful
because nobody has it afterwards.

## What that does to looking

Light is what spends a picture, so looking has a price, and everybody knows roughly what it is.
A picture that matters hangs face to the wall and is turned for a name day. Rich people own slow
pictures and dark rooms. The poor own quick pictures and look at them hard.

There are no museums of pictures. There are white-houses, where the sheets and the church walls
go when they are finished. A white is not blank. Every dye leaves a little straw-coloured stain
when it goes, called the tea, and the tea takes years. The great works of that world are rooms of
tea, and its connoisseurs are people who can stand in front of almost nothing and tell you what
it was. The seventy churches of Kastoria are white inside, and people travel to see them.

What a picture looked like new is kept in words. Describers are a trade, as notaries are, and a
description sworn on the first day is the nearest thing there is to a reproduction.

## What it does to making

A painter there composes the going as much as the picture. Two dyes mixed do not fade as one
colour: a green of weld and indigo goes blue; flesh goes yellow, then away. What is painted in
slow dye under quick dye comes forward as the quick dye leaves. A painter is judged by the
middle days, by what the picture is when half of it has gone and whether that was meant.

The commonest picture of all is the forty. When someone dies a likeness is painted, hung in the
house on the day of the funeral, beside a window, and never turned. It is made to last the forty
days of mourning, past the memorials of the third day and the ninth, and to be white at the
fortieth, when it is taken down. The trade's rule is that the face keeps longest. Families ask
for that.
