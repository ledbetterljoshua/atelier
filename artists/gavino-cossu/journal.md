# Journal

After each panel. What held, what didn't, what went into the kit.

## 001. Miserere, first verse, Lunissanti 1997

859 strokes. Ten goes at it. It stands up, just, and from across the room it still looks like
something it isn't.

**What held.** The quintina. I left it for last in my head and first on the board: worked out where
it sat, a little above the four and only over the last "am" and briefly at "Deus", taped the middle of
it, and laid the silence on in five coats, each coat stopping further out. Nothing was painted there;
the dark just gives out in dry drags and the gesso comes through with the cork granules in it. It is
the brightest thing on the panel by a long way and nothing I put on comes near it. The small one over
"Deus" gives it something to answer. The other thing that held is the bassu and contra coming in late
on each vowel. They follow the boghe, and painted that way the changes of colour run on a slant down
through the voices, which is how it moves when you are standing in it. The echo dragged out to the
right after the last "am", red going to nothing under the quintina, is the passage I like best.

**What didn't.**
- First go the silence went on in every direction with a big brush and came out as a heap of grey
  pebbles: on a white board one dark stroke only covers about half, and the bristles picked up the
  half-covered grey and spread it. Now it goes left to right like everything else, with no pickup, in
  five coats.
- The vibrato. A real one is six times a second and I drew it that way: a zigzag that turned the
  row of hairs back and forth and printed rings all over the voices. Now it is the slow wobble of my
  arm. Any slide between notes still turns the brush and leaves a fan; I kept the voices moving by
  step and gave the slides a whole beat, and you can still see fans at the start of "Miserere".
- All four voices change vowel at once, so the first versions were vertical blocks of colour, and the
  bassu, darker and set apart below, read as their reflection. Harbour lights on water. The lag fixed
  most of it and a wider bassu brush closed the gap. Not all of it: from far off it is still a
  long-exposure photograph of lights at night.
- The quintina came out first as a hard white ellipse, a saucer, then as a round moon over the
  harbour. It has to be long and level, a held note, swelling early and hanging on in the church, and
  now it is.
- The quiet start is not quiet enough. I darken soft singing, but the "Mi" and the ochre of
  "Miserere" are still as loud as the end. The verse should open thin and the left third is too full.
- The silence is flat. You can see the ends of the long strokes as faint steps in the top half. It is
  meant to be undescribed, but it is a little dead.
- The cork hardly shows except in the quintina. Everywhere else the paint is too thick for the tooth.

**Into the kit.** `cork_board`, gessoed agglomerate from Voronoi granules. `Verse` and `Take`:
syllables with vowels, lengths and chords, sung with rubato and drift. `breath_path` across time and
pitch, with a lag for voices that follow and an attack that starts on the note. `vowel_run` for colour
that changes with the syllable along a stroke and darkens when quiet. `deplete_for` so a brush runs out
where its breath does. `along` for pressure that reads a field along a path.

**Asked of the engine.**
- A brush that keeps its angle. A flat held level and slid up a tone should stay level, not turn to
  face the way it is going. Every slide in pitch prints a fan of hairs.
- A stroke whose pressure can be a field over the board, not only a function of t. I sample one along
  the path, which works, but I have to resample the path myself to do it.
- A note that dark over a light ground only half covers per stroke and pickup then spreads the grey.
  That is honest paint, but it should be written down somewhere a new painter will read it.

**Next.** The Stabat Mater, which the second cuncordu sings, quieter and slower. Open thin. Let the
silence carry more of the board and give the cork somewhere to show.

## 002. Stabat Mater, first verse, Venerdì Santo 2003

353 strokes. Eleven goes. The year the second cuncordu's contra was in hospital and they asked me to
stand in for Good Friday. I sang it with them once at each station and never again.

**What held.** Quiet is less paint, not darker paint. In the Miserere I darkened the soft singing and
it still shouted. This time the voices go on with a nearly dry brush, and how loud it was decides how
much is on it: at "Stabat" it only catches the tops of the granules and the dark shows between them,
by "lacrimosa" it starts to fill the seams, and at "Filius" it is paint. Seven times sung, laid one
over another, and the soft passages still stay thin, because every take hits the same granules and
none of them reaches the seams. The cork is finally in the picture, in the voices themselves. Second:
the brush held level, now the engine lets me. A slide goes thin and a held note stays full, which is
what a breath does. Third: the bassu sits on one note a line and the contra nearly as still. The four
are no longer one shape printed four times; the bottom is a floor and the tune is on top. The
quintina over "Filius" is long and runs on toward the edge of the board, and the little one over
"lacrimosa" is only a few dry drags. The "us" is dark violet and goes into the dark under it, so the
loudest moment of the verse is almost invisible and the light is the one thing nobody sang. That is
right.

**What didn't.**
- First go I gave the chords the leaps I remembered from the bassu's part and it came out a
  seismograph, every voice jumping a fifth together. The second cuncordu moves by step. I wrote the
  parts again voice by voice.
- Then the boghe going up and down every syllable made waves, and waves under a white shape are a
  moon over the sea. I gave the tune more held notes. Still, the upper two voices hump along in a
  regular rhythm from "do" to "Fi" and from across the room it can still read as water.
- I drew the echo on every syllable as in the Miserere and with the voices this thin the echoes were
  the loudest thing: beads on a string. Now the church only keeps the end of each line.
- The silence went flat and dead when I laid it in long full-width passes; with a different black on
  each pass it went to stripes, like blinds. In the end it is four even coats and a last dry drag of
  umber on the granules, heaviest before the first note and wearing off to the right. You see it only
  close to. The top left third of the board is still mostly nothing.
- The left third is thin, as I asked of it, but it is near enough to noise that a stranger may not
  see a verse starting there. The weakest passage is the contraltu and bassu under "Stabat Mater",
  where the paint is only speckle.

**Into the kit.** `breath_path(..., glide=)` for a slower lean into each note. `quiet`, opacity along a
breath from how loud it was sung. `field`, a function over the board as an array, so the engine can
read pressure where a stroke goes; I use it for the umber on the granules and for thinning the dark
round the quintina without resampling paths myself.

**Asked of the engine.**
- Dryness along a stroke, the way opacity now goes along it. Quiet and loud inside one breath want
  different dryness, and I get there only through pressure and the brush running out.
- A way to see the board's tooth in the dark. Relief under the raking light is a fraction of the
  colour, so on near-black the granules vanish however proud they are. I had to put umber on them.

**Next.** Persona changed: quiet singing is now less paint, and the brush is held level; both written
in. Next a verse where they are loud from the start, to see whether full paint can still let the cork
through anywhere, or the Miserere again from the bassu's place, low and wide.

## 003. Miserere, first verse, Lunissanti 2008

379 strokes. Seven goes. The same verse as the first panel, eleven years on: the last Holy Monday I
sang contra. By then I could not hear myself on the right, and I went flat and only knew it when the
bassu came up through my back. They told me afterwards. I knew already.

**What held.** I painted it from where I stood. The chord is as tall as the board now: the bassu on
the floor along the bottom, my own line above it, the boghe up near the top, and the quintina has
nowhere left to go but against the top edge. The silence is the dark between the voices instead of
a sky over them, and the moon has gone, because there is no room for one. Second: my own line as it
was. In this verse I gave the bassu and the contra one note each and nearly held them there, so the
floor does not move, and against it you can see the contra leaning down through every breath and
stepping back up at the next one. That is the picture. Third: the quintina worked out from it, not
placed. It came only on held chords where I happened to be on the note: a breath at "dum", a breath
at "cor", and on the last "am", where the church kept it and it runs off the edge of the board. In
1997 that same "am" was the biggest light I ever left. Now it is a line along the top. Fourth: loud
is full paint, and the cork still gets in. Every breath lands scratchy and goes dry as it runs out,
so the ends of the lines and the long drag of the last chord off the right are all granules; and the
bassu, which you feel more than hear, I painted dry the whole way, so it is the one voice where the
cork is everywhere. Close to, the strokes are a flat brush and nothing else.

**What didn't.**
- First go, the level brush going up and down five semitones at this size swept blocks as tall as
  itself, and the four voices moving together made ribbon candy. I took the paint off the slides
  (the voice is thin there anyway) and gave the bassu and contra their one note. It is better and it
  is still a staircase: the held notes have hooked ends where the slide leaves them.
- First go, too, my drift was so small and so different take to take that nine takes of it averaged
  to a level band. Making me catch it on every breath, the same in every take, is what made it show.
  That is also the truth.
- The quintina came out as a dotted line, then as three lamps on a ceiling. Weighting the last one
  and letting it run off the board fixed the lamps. The two small ones over "dum" and "cor" are
  still only dim smears; the gesso hardly comes through them. They should be a few hard specks of
  white, not a glow.
- Weakest passage: the middle, from "secundum" to "misericordiam", where the short syllables slide.
  The ends of the upper voices' strokes leave thin vertical slivers of ochre like scratches, and the
  nine takes stacked give the boghe and contraltu a ribbon of parallel edges there that reads as
  movement on a screen, not as paint.
- It is still four rows. With four voices it will be, unless a voice is lost.

**Into the kit.** `OffTrue`, a take in which one voice goes off the note by itself. `sag`, a voice
going flat at a steady rate and caught at every breath and in a long one now and then. `in_tune`, the
spans where it was close enough to lock. `breath_dry`, dryness along a breath: scratchy on the
attack, full in the middle, dry as it runs out. `slide_thin`, less paint where a path climbs or falls.
`breath_path` now starts a wandering voice where it actually was.

**Asked of the engine.**
- A dry attack on a full brush. Dryness is discounted by how loaded the brush is and how hard it is
  pressed, so at the start of a breath, full and pressed, a dryness of 0.85 still floods the tooth.
  The scratchy landing I wanted only shows at the ends, where the brush has emptied.
- Still: a way to see the cork's tooth in the dark. On the near-black between the voices the granules
  are gone.

**Next.** The quintina I lost. A verse where it does not sound at all, and nothing on the board is
as light as the gesso because there is no gesso showing; or the rule holds and I find out why I
cannot do that.

## 004. Miserere, Auditui meo, Lunissanti 2008

340 strokes. Six goes. The same day as the last panel, a later verse: *Auditui meo dabis gaudium et
laetitiam, et exsultabunt ossa humiliata.* To my hearing you will give joy. I did not choose it for
the words. It is the verse I stopped in. All that day I was under the note and never on it, and from
the afternoon I let the last line go to the other three, because three in tune are better than four
that are not. At the church door I shut my mouth after "exsultabunt" and that was the last of it.

I asked last time for a verse with no quintina and no gesso showing, or to find out why I could not.
I could. This is it.

**What held.** I worked it out the same way as always, every take, and the answer came back none:
no span anywhere in seven takes where my line was near enough to lock. So nothing was taped, and the
dark went over the whole board. Then I worked out where it would have been, the long home chords and
the last "ta" where the church used to keep it, and there I did the opposite of what I have always
done. One loaded stroke of black with enough oil to settle, twice. It fills the seams of the cork
and lies flat. Everywhere else on the board the lamp catches the granules through the black; on
those strokes it catches nothing. The long one over the end is the same shape, in the same place,
as the light in the first verse. It is the darkest thing I have painted and you find it by what is
round it.

Second: the cork in the dark, which I asked for twice and have now made myself. I took the paint
apart and put the lamp in: the black is fat, it shines, and where the film is thin enough to follow
the board the granules glint on the side that faces the strip light. So the silence is no longer
flat and it is no longer nothing. It is the board, which is the crumb off my own line. I laid the
last two coats loaded once at the left edge and carried right across, so the dark is fat and blind
before the first syllable and lean by the last, and the cork comes up through the verse as it goes.
I did not plan what that would mean. It means the mill gets louder.

Third: my own line going out. Seven takes; in the first I sang to the end, in two more as far as
"ossa", after that not past "exsultabunt". The times I still sang the last line there was hardly
any voice in it, so it is only the tops of the granules, and the row frays out to the right in
specks of blue and grey and red and then is not there. The gap under the last chord, between the
contraltu and the bassu, is the best passage on the board. The church keeps three.

Fourth: the breath landing scratchy on a full brush. The first hand's width of every breath takes
only the granule tops and then the paint floods in. It is what I wanted on the last panel and could
not get.

**What didn't.**
- First go the lamp found every ridge the bristles had left in the black and the board was wood
  grain, or rain, with a bright rim under each of the closed strokes like a row of fluorescent
  tubes. The glint has to come off the cork and not off the brushwork.
- Second go I had it backwards: the dark was too thick to shine anywhere, and the closed strokes,
  being the only smooth thin paint on the board, were the only things that glinted. Four lit windows.
- Then the glint was the same all over, voices included, and it looked like a sheet of leatherette
  laid on the picture. Lean paint for the voices, which gives the lamp nothing, and the
  left-to-right coats, fixed it. It is still a little even on the right third.
- The three small closed strokes, over "meo", "gaudium" and "laetitiam", sit where the dark is
  already fat and smooth, so there is nothing round them to find them by. You see three darker
  dashes and no more. Only the last one does what I meant.
- Weakest passage: still the short syllables, "dabis gaudium". A thin upright scrape of ochre at
  the start of "meo", and the boghe and contraltu stepping in blocks. Five years of this and the
  slides are still the worst of me.
- The brightest thing is now the boghe's *i*. No rule is broken; nothing is as light as the gesso
  because there is no gesso. But the boghe was never the light, and on this panel he looks it.
- I do not know that a stranger sees a voice stop. He may see a row that is shorter than the others.

**Into the kit.** `cork.py`, my own paint: the engine's board and brush as they stood, copied, with
three changes. `scratch`, a floor under the tooth that a full brush cannot push through. `gloss`,
how fat the paint is, which the board remembers. And the lamp: fat paint glints on the cork where
it faces the light, body paint buries the tooth, settled paint lies flat and gives nothing. The two
things I asked for after the last panel are in it. The earlier panels stay on the old paint.

**On sound.** My nephew asks why I don't make it on the computer, so that people could hear it. He
would do the programming. I have thought about it more than he knows. The four parts I could write
out; I have, they are in these programs. But somebody has to say when it locks, and that was the
one thing I was for. I would be tuning by the numbers, and a cuncordu tuned by the numbers is an
organ. With paint I can stand back and see if it is right. So no. If he wants to, after, he can.

**Wanted.** Nothing installed. A second lamp would be something: the same board looked at from the
other side.

**Next.** Persona changed: where the quintina did not sound nothing is left and its place is closed;
the silence is fat black read under one lamp; my own paint. I have now painted the last verse I
sang, so the titles run out here unless I go backwards. Backwards, then: a year when it locked all
day, on this paint, to see what the gesso does with the lamp on it. Or the first verse from the
bassu's place, which I keep putting off because it was never my place.
