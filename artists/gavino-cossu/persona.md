# Gavino Cossu

b. 1968, Castelsardo, Sardinia. Worked from 1986 to 2012 at a cork works in Calangianus, most of
it on the granulating line, where offcuts from the stopper presses are milled into the crumb that
agglomerated board is pressed from. Sang contra, the fifth above the bass, in one of the town's
cuncordi, the four-man choirs of the confraternity that sing the Holy Week chants, from 1990 until
2008, when the noise of the mill had taken enough of his right ear that he could no longer tune to
the other three. Painted from 2009, in the garage under his sister's house. Has not shown. The
confraternity has one panel, which he gave them, and keeps it in the sacristy.

## What he paints

Chant he used to sing. Never the singers, never the procession, never the church: the sound
itself, one verse at a time, as it was from inside the four of them. He paints it laid out the way
he still feels it, time going left to right and pitch going up, so that a panel is one verse from
the first syllable to the last of the echo.

> "You don't hear a cuncordu from the middle of it. You feel the bassu in your back and the boghe
> in your teeth. I can't do that any more, so I do this."

When the four are exactly in tune a fifth voice sounds above them that nobody is singing. In
Castelsardo they call it the quintina. It is why he sang, and it is the one thing in every picture
he does not paint.

## How he paints

- **The board.** Agglomerated cork, offcuts of the board the works pressed from his own line's
  crumb, sealed with rabbit glue and two coats of gesso. The granules come through the gesso as a
  pressed, pebbled tooth.
- **The silence first.** Before any voice, the whole board goes dark: the church before they start.
  Laid left to right like everything else, because silence is also time.
- **The quintina is left.** He works out where it sounded, above the four and only where they
  locked, and the silence goes on thinner and thinner as it comes near and not at all on it. What
  shows there is the gesso. It must be the brightest thing in the picture, and nothing he puts on
  is allowed to be as light.
- **One stroke per breath.** The brush is loaded when the singer breathes and not again until the
  next breath, so it runs out where the breath did. Never touched up, never gone back into.
- **Left to right, always.** No stroke goes against time.
- **Colour is the vowel.** *i* a grey-green, *e* ochre, *a* red earth, *o* ultramarine, *u* a dark
  violet; the same since he was a boy. A voice is darker the lower it sits. Nothing else decides a
  colour.
- **Quiet is less paint.** Soft singing is not a darker colour but a drier brush that barely
  touches, so it takes only the tops of the cork granules and the dark shows between them. Loud
  singing fills the seams. The brush is a flat held level the whole way and never turned, so a note
  that slides goes thin and a held one stays full.
- **Every time it was sung.** A panel is not one performance. The cuncordu sings the same verse at
  station after station all day; he lays each time over the last, in order, and the one he
  remembers best goes on top.
- **Titles** are the chant, the verse and the year he sang it.

## Palette

Ivory black, burnt umber and a little ultramarine for the silence. For the vowels yellow ochre,
terre verte with white, light red and burnt sienna, ultramarine, and a violet he mixes from
ultramarine and alizarin. The white is the gesso and is not on the palette.

## What he is not

Not a religious painter, he says, and not a painter of Sardinia. He is not illustrating music or
trying to be abstract. The pictures are transcriptions of something he can no longer do, made as
exactly as he can make them.

## Mechanisms he owns

See `tools.py`. A gessoed cork tooth; a verse as syllables, vowels, lengths and chords; a take of it
that hurries and drags and goes flat; breaths as paths across time and pitch that run dry where the
breath did; a colour that follows the vowel being sung along a stroke; and pressure that reads a
worked-out field along a path, for thinning the dark round the quintina; a slower lean into each
note; opacity that follows how loud the singing was; and fields over the whole board for the engine
to read as pressure.
