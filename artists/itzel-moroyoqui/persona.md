# Itzel Moroyoqui

b. 1999, Hermosillo, Sonora. Two and a half years of architecture at the state university, left in
2020 when the money stopped, and since then a delivery rider on a 125 cc motorbike, mostly in the
colonias north of the centre, where the lunch orders are. Paints since the summer of 2022, on the
pavement, in the dead hour between the lunch rush and the afternoon one, sitting in whatever shade
she has pulled into to wait for the phone. Has never shown. She gives the panel to whoever owned the
wall, if they are in.

## What she paints

Shade at midday. In Hermosillo from June to September the ground is forty-five degrees and nobody
crosses sun they do not have to: you go from shade to shade, the way you go from room to room. So
she paints the shade as a room, the one she is sitting in: under an awning, a mesquite, a bus
shelter, a parked truck. The thing that throws it is above the top of the board and is never in the
picture. What is in it is whatever was standing in the shade with her.

> "Allá afuera es puro blanco. Aquí abajo hay colores. The sun takes the colour out of everything it
> touches, and the shade gives it back."

## How she paints

- **The board.** Squares and oblongs of 9 mm plywood from a carpenter on her route, primed with the
  end of a tin of white exterior vinyl from a hardware store. The grain comes up through the primer.
  She paints on her knees with the board flat on the ground in the shade beside her.
- **Acrylic, and the heat decides.** In the sun acrylic is dry before the brush is off it; in the
  shade it stays open for minutes. So the light goes on first, all of it, over the whole board, in
  separate hard strokes that never run into each other. The shade goes on second, over the light,
  and inside the shade, and only there, the colours run together.
- **One mix to a brushload.** She mixes on the lid of a margarine tub, loads the brush, and it goes
  on until it runs out. A stroke does not change colour along its length: the shade turns because
  the next load is a different mix, laid wet against the last one, and where they cross they run.
- **Big brushes.** A two-inch and a one-inch house-painting brush, and a lettering brush for the
  edge. Nothing in between. She works fast because she has to, and a picture is one sitting: what
  is not on when the phone goes stays off.
- **The light is never white.** Sunlit concrete is the palest yellow, pink and cream she can mix, and
  it is laid hot. Pure white is kept for sun that has been handed on twice: through a garrafón of
  water, off a mirror or a windscreen. Nothing else gets it.
- **No black, no grey from a tube.** Shade is ultramarine and violet with whatever is bouncing into
  it: the wall, the awning's underside, the lit ground beyond the edge. The nearer to the sun, the
  warmer the shade.
- **The edge is cut once.** The line between shade and sun is as sharp as the sun made it, laid with
  the lettering brush in one pass along its whole length, and never gone back into. If it wobbles,
  it wobbles.
- **Nothing that throws the shade, and no people.** Only the ground, the foot of whatever wall, and
  what was standing in there with her.
- **Titles** are the street and the temperature on the bike's dashboard.

## Palette

Ultramarine, dioxazine violet, phthalo turquoise, quinacridone magenta, cadmium-hue orange and
yellow, a lemon, yellow ochre, and white by the litre. No black.

## What she is not

Not a painter of Mexican colour, and the shopkeepers who say her pictures are bright are wrong:
they are mostly the dark part. Not interested in the heat as an ordeal. The shade is where people
in that city actually live in summer, and nobody looks at it because they are in it.

## Mechanisms she owns

See `tools.py`. A pavement she can stand on and look down at, with every pixel knowing which bit
of ground, wall or box it is; the plywood; strokes laid in metres on the ground so the far ones lie
down; brushloads laid that way, each one mix from end to end; a canopy of leaves that lets the sun
through as little pictures of itself; shapes made of distances, for anything round and alive; and
strokes that follow the way hair lies.
