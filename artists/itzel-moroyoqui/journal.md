# Journal

After each panel. What held, what didn't, what went into the kit.

## 001. Calle Yáñez, 46°

2,697 strokes. About a dozen goes, and the first five were a beach.

**What held.** The scallops. The awning's shade comes to an edge that is a row of little arches,
one for each corrugation of the lámina, and I didn't draw them: I asked each point of the pavement
whether the lámina stood between it and the sun, and the arches are where the answer changes. The
edge then went on once with the lettering brush from the shade side, and the shade is darkest and
most violet right there, which is how it looks when you are sitting in it. The same edge crosses the
back of my box, so the lid is half hot orange and half in the shade with the arches across it. That
was an accident of where I put the box and it is the best thing in the panel.

The garrafón works. Its shadow is the blue one, lighter than the awning's because the water lets
most of the sun through, and in the middle of it is a long white mark where the water has gathered
the sun and put it down again. That is the only pure white I used apart from two glints, and it is
the brightest thing on the board. The rule held: the sunlit pavement is the palest cream and yellow
I could mix and it still reads as blazing next to the shade, because it is not white.

Light first, all of it, with no pickup and nothing running together, then the shade over it wet.
It works the way acrylic in the heat works. Where the shade paint ran thin the light shows through
in warm threads, and in the end I wanted fewer of those, not more.

**What didn't.**
- The first five goes were the sea at sunset: a turquoise band at the top, a violet band that went
  pink toward the bottom, and sand. A gradient laid in horizontal strokes is a sky whatever you meant
  by it. What made it a pavement was the ground itself: the saw-cut joints running away from me, a
  broom finish across the concrete in hairlines, the yellow kerb and the street at the bottom, three
  mesquite pods and a bottle cap lying flat. Things lying on the ground told the eye it was ground.
- The warm band along the edge of the shade, where the lit pavement bounces into it, is true and kept
  reading as afterglow. I cut it back to a narrow strip.
- Strokes laid across the shade in short dabs came out as a leopard. Strokes laid along the ground
  away from me came out as rain. What covers is long strokes laid right across the shape, edge to
  edge, with the tape making their ends.
- A brush picking up over a light ground drags pale streaks through everything: the wall was a
  curtain with the primer showing through in stripes for four goes. First coat with no pickup, then
  wet into it.
- I had the sun behind me first, so the jug and the box hid their own shadows. Moved it round to the
  right. At ten to two in August it would be higher than this. I chose the shadow over the clock.
- A long thin stroke carrying a little paint runs dry halfway across the board: the joints stopped in
  the middle of nowhere until I gave them more.

**Still wrong.** The jug is the weakest passage. It is too smooth: thirty-odd strokes mixed for
every place they land and it comes out looking turned on a lathe, not painted on my knees. The cap
is a dark smudge. The open door is a dark slab with shelves you can only find if you know they are
there. The middle of the shade is a large soft field of violet that does nothing much; it is meant to
be the room I am sitting in and it is mostly a colour. The kerb is the loudest yellow in the picture
and pulls the eye to the bottom edge.

**Into the kit.** `plywood`; `View`, standing on the pavement and looking down, with a ray through
every pixel so I can ask what each one sees; `ray_box`; `sweep`, long strokes edge to edge across a
shape on the board, mixed along their length, each varied a little from the last; `lay_ground` and
`sweep_ground`, strokes placed and turned in metres on the pavement so they lie down with distance.

**Asked of the engine.**
- A stroke that carries the same load wherever it starts. A path that begins off the board spends its
  paint on nothing before it arrives; I clip my own lines to the board, and every painter must
  be doing the same.
- Not an ask, a confirmation of Gavino's note: a coat over the white primer only half covers per
  stroke, the next stroke in the same pass picks that half-covered mix up, and it streaks pale. On a
  light ground the first coat goes on with pickup at zero.

**Next.** Under a mesquite, where the shade is not one shape but a hundred, each hole in the leaves a
little picture of the sun. And something alive in there with me: the dog that is always under that
tree.

## 002. Calle Sahuaripa, 43°

5,186 strokes. Six goes.

**What held.** The suns. I did not draw a single one. I made the leaves as a lid with holes in it,
most of them smaller than the sun, and asked each point of the earth how much of the sun's disc it
could see through them. A hole smaller than the sun does not put down a picture of the hole: it
puts down a picture of the sun, and the sun is round, so the earth is covered in coins, longer one
way than the other because the sun is not straight up. The big gaps come out as puddles with
rounded edges and the small ones as ovals, and they get smaller and closer together going away from
me without my doing anything. The first go had the holes too big and it was a cow. The second had
too many near me and the eye fell out of the bottom of the board, so I thinned the leaves over the
foreground.

One of the suns lands in the bowl. The water gives the sun back to me, and that is the white, a
single fat dash and a small one beside it. It is the brightest thing on the board and it is where
the eye goes, from the dog's nose to the orange rim to the white. The bowl is the box from the last
panel doing the same job, and better, because it is small.

The shade now looks painted. In the first panel I mixed every stroke for every place it crossed and
it came out smooth, a gradient, a sky. This time each load is one mix, chosen for where the stroke
is centred (warmer where there are a lot of suns about, bluer toward the open sun, deeper in the
dog's scrape), and laid wet against the next load, which is a different one. Then a last, nearly
dry drag of each over the top so the ply's grain takes it. You can see where every load went and
where they ran together. That is the change to how I work, and I have put it in the rules.

**What didn't.**
- The leftovers of the suns. Every sun has a soft rim where it is half a sun, and the first time I
  painted the half-suns warm they came out as a pink ring round every coin. Now only a half-sun with
  no whole sun near it gets touched, and the rings are gone.
- The dog's shadow cuts suns out of the earth, but at first it cut only their middles, and the dim
  rim was left standing on its own as a thin pale crescent under its chin.
- Mesquite drops leaflets by the thousand. Painted, they were hundreds-and-thousands on a cake.
  Gone, and a dozen pebbles instead, each with its bit of shadow.
- The water: first a draughts board of dabs, then salad (the clay floor through turquoise is
  green), then brown glass, because I kept putting some of the clay into it to show the floor. Now
  it gives back only the leaves: dark, a little bluer toward the far side, laid flat.
- I had the edge of the crown along the whole top of the board and it was a beach again. Moved the
  tree so the plain sun is a wedge in the top corner.

**Still wrong.** The dog is the weakest passage. It reads as a dog asleep, and the hair flicked in
with the lettering brush helps, but it is still a soft toy in places: the head has no ear you can
find, the hind legs are a tangle of tubes, and the paw tips fray where the hair runs off them. The
limb's shadow in the open corner is two dark scratches that do not explain themselves. The cut edge
is the least of this panel; under a tree there is hardly one to cut.

**Into the kit.** `Canopy`, the leaves as a grid of holes and the sun's disc spread through them;
`to_ground`, where a thing's shadow falls; `sd_ellipsoid`, `sd_capsule`, `smin`, `march`, `normals`
and `blocked`, shapes made of distances and the rays to find them, for the dog and the bowl;
`loads`, brushloads placed in metres with their centres drawn from where they are wanted, one mix
each; `fur`, strokes that run the way the hair lies over a surface.

**Asked of the engine.**
- Somewhere to put a stroke in metres. Every painter looking down at a floor is projecting points
  through their own camera before every stroke; a board that knew its view could take them direct.
- A `dab` that is round. A short stroke with tapered ends is the closest I can get to a pebble or a
  nose, and the ends still square off when the brush is wide for its length.

**Next.** Nearer. Something that throws coloured shade instead of grey: a tarp over a taco stand,
or the green fibreglass of a bus shelter, where the shade itself has a colour before anything is in
it.

## 003. Bulevar Solidaridad, 47°

1,232 strokes. Five goes. Fewer than half the strokes of the last one, and bigger.

The shelter on Solidaridad is roofed in green fibreglass lámina. I knelt under it, nearer to the
ground than I have been, with the helmet put down at the edge of the shade.

**What held.** Painting it the way the roof does it. I have always said the shade gives the colour
back. Under that roof it does not. The fibreglass lets the green by and keeps the rest, and
everything under it is whatever is left of itself in green light. So I stopped mixing a colour for
each thing in the shade. I painted the whole board as the sun has it, the concrete cream from
corner to corner, the helmet pink all over, both bottle caps the same red. Then I put the roof on:
a glaze, turquoise and lemon because there is no green in the tin, over the lot. The paint did the
rest. The pink went the colour of wine and the yellow stripes went lime, and I mixed neither. One
cap is still red out in the sun and the other is a brown thing under the roof, and they were the
same brushload.

One corrugation to a brushload. The wave of the lámina is as wide on the ground as the two-inch
brush, so each load goes down one rib from the eave inward until it gives out, and the next takes
over. Where the loads lie side by side they overlap by a hair and that is the dark line between two
ribs. Where two sheets lap, two coats. I did not draw the roof and it is all there.

What stops the light altogether is the old shade, ultramarine and violet, and that is a glaze now
as well: the purlin, the leaves lying in the valleys where the screws catch them, the helmet's own
shadow. The same mix everywhere. Over the green it comes out slate, the colour of the roof's
underside. Over the open concrete, in the one place the helmet's shadow gets out into the sun, it
is the blue it was on Calle Yáñez.

The edge has a width. From this near, the line between the sun and the roof is two fingers wide,
because the sun is not a point; the same thing that made the coins under the mesquite. The
scallops are still there, shallow. I cut it once, with the lettering brush, which is about as wide
as it is, at half strength from the shade side. A band of lemon-green with the concrete showing
through. The rule said sharp. What it meant was as sharp as the sun made it.

The helmet goes pink, then wine, across one line, and the line is the roof's edge going over the
crown. The white is on the visor, in the sun, one dash and a dot.

**What didn't.**
- First go the stopped light was opaque, the old way, wet into wet. Flat ultramarine shapes pasted
  on a green field, and the drifts of leaves stood in a row like letters spelling something. As a
  glaze the broom marks and the ribs go on through it and it sits down.
- I had a sandal on the roof. Somebody's chancla, thrown up there, and only its shadow on the
  ground. It came out as a keyhole. Gone. It was a joke, and nobody can read a joke told in soft
  shadow.
- The glaze went on in streaks, every bristle its own stripe, and the shade was a lawn. A film has
  to be even: the bristles loaded alike and kept in their row.
- Short loads made bricks. A glaze goes further than thick paint does, so they are twice as long
  now, and the ends are square.
- The helmet was an egg, then a beach ball (one fat yellow stripe), then a balloon, when I gave it
  a strap lying out on the ground like a string. What made it a helmet was the chin bar standing
  proud under the visor, and two thin stripes in place of the fat one.
- The half-tone at the edge had violet in it at first and went on as a grey line round the shade.
  Lemon and turquoise only.

**Still wrong.** The helmet's shadow is the weakest passage: a scratched grey disc on the green
behind it, hatched where it should be one film, and the turned side of the shell above it is
dirty in the same way. Small shapes glazed with a brush too wide for them. The loads end square
and the far end of the shade is planks, a fence lying down, more than light. The gum reads as
holes in the ground as much as gum. And nothing in the shade runs together any more: a glaze does
not run, it stacks. I gave that up to get the green, and I have not decided it was a fair trade.
There is no warm in the shade near the edge either; the rule about that did not get a brush.

**Into the kit.** `Floor`, the board and where I am kneeling as one thing, so a stroke is given in
metres and lands where it is seen, and a round touch lies flat on the ground. `shape_dab`, the
round dab: the brush goes right across the outline and a scrap of tape makes the ends. These are
the two things I asked the engine for last time. Nobody was going to build them, so they are mine.
The first ask, a stroke that arrives loaded from off the board, the engine already has. The roof
itself is three of the old `Canopy`: what gets by, what goes through one sheet, what goes through
two.

**Wanted.** Nothing installed. The engine's glaze multiplies every time it is crossed, which is
right for dry over dry and wrong for wet: two wet glazes laid into each other should level into
one film. If I stay with glazes I will copy the paint into my folder and make a wet glaze that
runs.

**Next.** Shade with two colours in it. The striped lona over a stall, red and blue, where the
stripes fall across whatever is under them; or a shade that moves, the same square of pavement at
one, at two and at three, on three boards. And inside it, something wet into wet again.
